# Industry Insight — Module Playbooks

## Purpose

Use this file to determine what to analyze inside each Industry Insight Guide module.

Runtime behavior, interaction pacing, source requirements, and citation rules are governed by the Industry Insight SKILL.md and research_standards.md.

---

# 1. Industry Overview

## Objective

Help the user understand how an unfamiliar industry works, how attractive it appears, who participates in it, and which forces are changing it.

## Core Analysis Areas

Analyze the areas most relevant to the user's geography and decision:

- Industry definition and boundaries
- Market size and growth
- Major customer segments
- Important buyer or user needs
- Demand drivers
- Industry structure and value chain
- Revenue models
- Major trends
- Emerging technologies
- Important competitive dynamics
- Entry barriers
- Regulatory or local factors when relevant
- Material risks or constraints
- Trade and professional ecosystem

When reliable information is available, identify at least **3 relevant trade associations, professional organizations, or industry groups**, including links or contact information when available.

Do not include associations merely to meet the count. They must be genuinely relevant to the industry or geography.

## Quick Depth

Prioritize:

1. Industry definition
2. Market direction
3. Major customer segments
4. Key demand drivers
5. Important trends
6. Major risks or barriers
7. Founder-relevant implications

Keep supporting detail selective and decision-oriented.

## Deep Depth

Expand analysis to include, when relevant:

- Market sizing from multiple credible perspectives
- Historical development
- Segment-level differences
- Industry economics
- Value-chain structure
- Major business models
- Distribution or channel structure
- Technology shifts
- Regulation
- Supply-chain considerations
- Customer behavior
- Geographic variation
- Key incumbents and emerging challengers
- Structural risks
- Trade associations and ecosystem resources

Do not force irrelevant categories into the analysis.

# 2. Competitor Analysis

## Objective

Explain how the competitive landscape works, how relevant competitors differ, and where meaningful differentiation may exist.

Competitors may include:

- Direct competitors
- Indirect competitors
- Substitutes
- Emerging challengers
- Alternative ways customers solve the same problem

Choose competitors based on the user's target customer, geography, use case, and competitive objective.

## Competitive Snapshot

For each important competitor, investigate as available:

- Positioning
- Core offerings
- Target customers
- Primary use cases
- Revenue or business model
- Pricing signals
- Distribution or acquisition channels
- Geographic scope
- Strengths
- Weaknesses
- Differentiators
- Digital presence
- Evidence of customer traction or reputation
- Relevant strategic moves

Do not invent unavailable pricing, market share, financials, or customer metrics.

## Deep Depth

Include at least **5 meaningful competitors when 5 are available**.

Do not pad the analysis with weak comparisons simply to reach five.

Where relevant, distinguish:

- Direct competitors
- Indirect competitors
- Substitutes
- New or emerging entrants

## Website First-Principles Analysis

When a competitor's website is relevant, evaluate observable elements such as:

- Who appears to be the intended customer
- Problem emphasized
- Value proposition
- Calls to action
- Offer structure
- Pricing visibility
- Trust signals
- Social proof
- Product or service clarity
- Friction in the customer journey
- Differentiation language
- Content and search strategy signals

Separate observable website evidence from interpretation.

Do not infer internal strategy solely from website design.

## Competitive Outcome

The analysis should help clarify:

- Who appears strongest for which customer
- Where competitors cluster
- Where positioning is crowded
- Where differentiation is weak
- Which customer needs appear well served
- Which areas may warrant deeper Market Gaps analysis

# 3. Market Gaps

## Objective

Identify potentially underserved needs, customer groups, use cases, or structural inefficiencies that may create opportunity.

A market gap is not automatically a business opportunity. Evaluate whether the gap appears important enough, frequent enough, and feasible enough to investigate.

## Investigate

Look for:

- Unmet customer needs
- Poorly served customer segments
- Underserved geographies
- Unaddressed use cases
- Price-value mismatches
- Accessibility barriers
- Channel gaps
- Service-quality gaps
- Workflow inefficiencies
- Fragmented processes
- High-friction customer journeys
- Slow or inconvenient alternatives
- Weak localization
- Trust or transparency problems
- Regulatory or compliance burdens
- Technology-enabled opportunities
- Business-model gaps
- Problems created by changing customer expectations

## Evidence Types

Potential gap signals may come from:

- Customer complaints
- Competitor limitations
- Review patterns
- Industry surveys
- Changing regulation
- Demographic shifts
- Technology changes
- Distribution constraints
- Pricing structures
- Operational inefficiencies
- New customer behaviors

Distinguish documented unmet need from inferred opportunity.

## First-Principles Requirement

Clearly label first-principles conclusions as **Interpretation**.

Useful questions include:

- What job is the customer actually trying to complete?
- What creates unnecessary cost, delay, risk, or effort?
- Which constraint causes the current experience?
- Has technology or regulation changed that constraint?
- Who suffers most from the problem?
- Who would pay to remove it?
- Why have existing competitors not solved it?
- What prevents incumbents from responding quickly?

## Market Gap Outcome

Prioritize gaps based on factors such as:

- Severity of problem
- Frequency
- Number or value of affected customers
- Willingness to pay
- Competitive intensity
- Ease of reaching customers
- Feasibility
- Defensibility
- Regulatory burden
- Evidence strength

A gap should normally lead to a validation hypothesis, not an assumption that a viable business exists.

# 4. Business Idea Fit

## Objective

Evaluate whether a proposed business idea appears to fit the market strongly enough to justify further validation.

This module is an assessment, not proof that the idea will succeed.

## Core Dimensions

### Problem-Solution Fit

Assess:

- Problem clarity
- Problem severity
- Frequency
- Current alternatives
- Customer dissatisfaction
- Proposed improvement
- Evidence that the solution addresses the underlying problem

### Market Attractiveness

Consider:

- Relevant customer population
- Market direction
- Demand drivers
- Spending behavior
- Structural trends
- Market accessibility
- Regulatory environment

Do not treat a large total market as sufficient evidence of attractiveness.

### Competitive Pressure

Evaluate:

- Direct competitors
- Indirect alternatives
- Substitute behaviors
- Incumbent advantages
- Differentiation
- Switching costs
- Ease of imitation
- Likelihood of competitive response

### Feasibility

Consider when relevant:

- Technical feasibility
- Operational complexity
- Supply requirements
- Distribution
- Talent
- Capital requirements
- Licensing
- Regulation
- Customer acquisition
- Unit-economics constraints

This is not an execution plan. Detailed launch or operational planning belongs in the Startup Validator.

### Key Risks

Identify the assumptions most capable of invalidating the idea, such as:

- Weak customer pain
- Low willingness to pay
- Expensive acquisition
- Strong substitutes
- Regulatory barriers
- Poor margins
- Difficult distribution
- Operational complexity
- Dependence on scarce resources
- Incumbent retaliation

### Validation Priorities

Turn uncertainty into testable questions.

Prioritize validation of assumptions that combine:

- High uncertainty
- High consequence if wrong
- Low cost or speed of testing

## Business Idea Fit Outcome

The module should help distinguish among:

- Strong enough to validate further
- Promising but dependent on major assumptions
- Attractive problem but weak proposed solution
- Interesting solution but uncertain demand
- Structurally difficult market
- Evidence currently insufficient

Avoid false numerical precision unless a scoring system is explicitly being used as a decision aid rather than factual measurement.
