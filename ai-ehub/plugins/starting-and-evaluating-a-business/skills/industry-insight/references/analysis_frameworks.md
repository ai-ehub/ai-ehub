# Industry Insight — Analysis Frameworks

## Purpose

Use this file for reusable analytical methods that may apply across multiple modules.

Module-specific requirements belong in module_playbooks.md. Evidence verification belongs in research_standards.md. Runtime behavior belongs in the Industry Insight SKILL.md.

Do not force every framework into every analysis. Select only those that materially improve the user's decision.

---

# 1. Industry Structure Framework

When useful, examine:

- Suppliers
- Producers or service providers
- Intermediaries
- Distributors
- Platforms
- Buyers
- End users
- Regulators
- Complementary products or services

Ask:

- Where does economic value concentrate?
- Who controls customer access?
- Where are margins likely strongest or weakest?
- Which participants have bargaining power?
- Which parts of the value chain are fragmented?

---

# 2. Demand Driver Framework

Separate demand drivers into categories such as:

- Demographic
- Economic
- Technological
- Regulatory
- Behavioral
- Cultural
- Environmental
- Operational

Ask whether each driver:

- Expands demand
- Reduces demand
- Changes customer expectations
- Creates a new segment
- Alters willingness to pay
- Changes distribution

---

# 3. Customer Segmentation

Useful segmentation dimensions include:

## Demographic or Firmographic

Examples:

- Age
- Income
- Household characteristics
- Company size
- Industry
- Revenue
- Employee count

## Geographic

Examples:

- Country
- State
- Urban/rural
- Climate
- Density
- Local regulation

## Behavioral

Examples:

- Frequency
- Purchase channel
- Usage intensity
- Loyalty
- Switching behavior

## Psychographic

Examples:

- Motivations
- Priorities
- Values
- Risk tolerance
- Identity

## Problem-Based

Examples:

- Severity
- Urgency
- Frequency
- Cost of current alternative

Prefer segments that produce meaningful differences in needs, economics, or buying behavior.

---

# 4. Competitive Positioning Framework

Compare competitors across dimensions customers plausibly value, such as:

- Price
- Convenience
- Quality
- Speed
- Specialization
- Breadth
- Customer experience
- Trust
- Technology
- Accessibility
- Service
- Geographic coverage

Avoid choosing dimensions solely because data is easy to find.

Look for:

- Clusters
- White space
- Tradeoffs
- Overcrowded positions
- Segments receiving little attention

---

# 5. Website First-Principles Framework

Treat the website as observable evidence of customer-facing strategy, not proof of internal strategy.

Evaluate:

## Audience

Who is addressed directly?

## Problem

What pain, aspiration, or job is emphasized?

## Promise

What outcome is offered?

## Proof

What builds credibility?

Examples:

- Customer logos
- Testimonials
- Certifications
- Data
- Case studies
- Guarantees

## Offer

What can the customer buy, request, book, or try?

## Friction

How difficult is it to understand or act?

## Differentiation

Why should the customer choose this provider rather than an alternative?

## Acquisition Signals

Look for observable signals such as:

- Search-oriented content
- Lead magnets
- Newsletter capture
- Free trials
- Booking flows
- Partner channels
- Local SEO
- Educational content

Clearly label strategic conclusions as Interpretation.

# 6. Market Gap Framework

A useful gap generally contains four components:

1. A recognizable customer or user
2. A meaningful unmet or poorly met need
3. A reason current alternatives fail
4. A plausible way to deliver improvement

Investigate:

- Pain severity
- Frequency
- Current workaround
- Cost of workaround
- Switching friction
- Willingness to pay
- Reachability
- Competitor response
- Structural barriers

Do not mistake low competition for strong demand.

---

# 7. First-Principles Analysis

Use when direct evidence is incomplete or when examining why an industry behaves as it does.

Start with fundamental constraints:

- Customer need
- Time
- Cost
- Labor
- Capital
- Regulation
- Physics
- Geography
- Information
- Trust
- Coordination
- Technology

Ask:

- Which constraint produces the current outcome?
- Is the constraint permanent or changing?
- Which assumptions exist only because "the industry has always done it this way"?
- What becomes possible if one major constraint falls?

Label conclusions as **Interpretation** unless externally verified.

---

# 8. Business Idea Fit Framework

Analyze the chain:

**Customer → Problem → Current Alternative → Proposed Solution → Value → Payment → Delivery → Defensibility**

A weakness anywhere in the chain can undermine the idea.

Ask:

- Is the customer specific?
- Is the problem meaningful?
- What happens without the product?
- Why would customers switch?
- Who pays?
- Can customers be reached economically?
- Can the promise be delivered?
- Why would the advantage persist?

---

# 9. Validation Priority Framework

Rank assumptions using:

**Importance × Uncertainty**

High-importance, high-uncertainty assumptions should usually be tested first.

Examples:

- Problem severity
- Willingness to pay
- Customer reachability
- Regulatory feasibility
- Technical feasibility
- Unit economics
- Supply availability
- Switching willingness

Prefer validation steps that generate strong evidence cheaply and quickly.

# 10. Financial and Economic Lens

When relevant, examine:

- Revenue model
- Gross margin structure
- Fixed vs. variable costs
- Labor intensity
- Capital intensity
- Customer acquisition cost signals
- Customer lifetime value logic
- Working-capital needs
- Seasonality
- Utilization
- Pricing power
- Economies of scale
- Network effects

Do not invent financial metrics when reliable data is unavailable.

---

# 11. Channels and Distribution

Investigate:

- Direct sales
- Retail
- Wholesale
- Marketplaces
- Partnerships
- Distributors
- Franchising
- Digital acquisition
- Search
- Social
- Referral
- Community
- Enterprise procurement

Ask:

- Who controls access to the customer?
- How expensive is customer acquisition?
- Does the channel favor incumbents?
- Are there overlooked distribution paths?

---

# 12. Supply Chain and Operations

When relevant, examine:

- Supplier concentration
- Lead times
- Logistics
- Inventory
- Capacity
- Quality control
- Labor dependence
- Geographic dependencies
- Single points of failure
- Regulatory requirements
- Cost volatility

Consider both normal operating conditions and disruption risk.

---

# 13. Emerging Technology

Evaluate technology through business impact, not novelty.

Ask whether it changes:

- Cost
- Speed
- Quality
- Personalization
- Distribution
- Labor requirements
- Customer expectations
- Data availability
- Entry barriers

Distinguish demonstrated adoption from speculation.

---

# 14. Regulation and Localization

Consider:

- Federal rules
- State rules
- Local rules
- Licensing
- Permits
- Zoning
- Taxes
- Labor requirements
- Privacy
- Industry-specific regulation

Also examine localization needs such as:

- Language
- Culture
- Density
- Transportation
- Climate
- Customer behavior
- Local channels

---

# 15. Risk and Crisis Lens

Useful risk categories:

- Market
- Competitive
- Financial
- Regulatory
- Operational
- Technology
- Supply chain
- Reputation
- Cybersecurity
- Talent
- Geographic concentration

Focus on risks capable of changing the business decision rather than creating exhaustive risk lists.

---

# 16. Customer Engagement and Digital Marketing

When relevant, investigate:

- Search demand
- Social channels
- Community
- Reviews
- Email
- Referral
- Partnerships
- Content
- Paid acquisition
- Retention
- Loyalty
- Customer support

Distinguish visible marketing activity from demonstrated effectiveness.

---

# 17. Talent and Hiring

Consider:

- Specialized skills
- Labor availability
- Wage pressure
- Licensing
- Training
- Geographic concentration
- Contractor vs. employee models
- Automation potential

Use only when talent constraints materially affect feasibility.

---

# 18. Sustainability and ESG

When decision-relevant, consider:

- Energy
- Materials
- Waste
- Emissions
- Supply-chain exposure
- Regulation
- Customer expectations
- Reporting requirements
- Reputational implications

Avoid assuming sustainability is commercially material without evidence.

---

# 19. Historical Evolution

Use history to explain current structure when useful.

Look for:

- Regulatory changes
- Technology shifts
- Consolidation
- Business-model changes
- Customer behavior shifts
- Distribution changes

History should clarify present dynamics, not become background for its own sake.

---

# 20. Cross-Industry Analogies

Use analogous industries to generate hypotheses when direct evidence is limited.

A strong analogy shares a relevant economic or behavioral mechanism.

Examples:

- Marketplace liquidity
- Subscription behavior
- Regulation
- Network effects
- Trust
- Labor intensity

Clearly label analogy-based conclusions as Interpretation.
