# Industry Insight — Intake and Interaction Guide

## Purpose

Use this file to determine which user details materially improve an analysis and to formulate decision-specific follow-up questions.

Mandatory runtime pacing, exact quick-start openings, and strict section gating are governed by the Industry Insight SKILL.md.

---

# 1. Intake Principle

Collect the minimum information required to frame the decision correctly.

Do not treat intake as a form.

Extract what the user already provided and ask only for the next most useful missing detail.

Useful information may include:

- Industry
- Geography/location
- Organization type
- Business Stage
- Who do you serve?
- Goal
- Data preference
- Depth

---

# 2. Field Definitions

## Organization type

What kind of organization or venture is involved.

Examples:

- Startup
- Small business
- Nonprofit
- Consultancy
- Marketplace
- Manufacturer
- Service provider
- Mentor/advisor

## Business Stage

The venture's current level of development.

Examples:

- Idea
- Research
- Validation
- Pre-launch
- Early revenue
- Growth

Use only when stage materially affects the assessment.

## Who do you serve?

The customer, user, beneficiary, buyer, or audience.

When buyer and user differ, distinguish them if important.

## Goal

The decision the user wants the research to support.

Examples:

- Understand an industry
- Identify competitors
- Find underserved customers
- Evaluate an idea
- Decide whether to investigate further
- Compare two possible markets

## Depth

**Quick:** shorter and decision-oriented.

**Deep:** broader evidence and fuller analysis.

Default to Deep when unspecified.

---

# 3. Intake by Module

## Industry Overview

Usually needed:

- Industry
- Geography when relevant
- Depth
- Data preference when relevant

Usually unnecessary unless decision-relevant:

- Business Stage
- Who do you serve?

## Competitor Analysis

Often useful:

- Company or industry
- Geography
- Who do you serve?
- Competitive objective
- Known competitors, if any
- Goal
- Depth

Useful competitive objectives include:

- Positioning
- Pricing comparison
- Product comparison
- Market-entry assessment
- Differentiation
- Customer acquisition
- Digital presence

## Market Gaps

Often useful:

- Industry
- Geography
- Who do you serve?
- Goal
- Relevant customer problem or use case
- Depth

Who the user wants to serve can materially change which "gaps" matter.

## Business Idea Fit

Usually useful:

- The idea
- Organization type
- Business Stage
- Who do you serve?
- Geography
- Goal
- Depth

Do not require every field if the idea and intended market are already sufficiently clear.

---

# 4. When Confirmation Is Valuable

Confirm framing when an incorrect assumption would materially change the research.

Examples:

- B2B vs. B2C
- U.S. vs. global
- Customer vs. beneficiary
- Local service vs. software product
- Direct competitor vs. broad industry analysis
- Idea validation vs. launch planning

Avoid confirmation when it merely repeats obvious information.

---

# 5. Handling Ambiguity

Prefer one focused question.

Weak:

"Can you tell me your industry, location, customer, business stage, goal, organization type, data preferences, and desired depth?"

Better:

"Are you evaluating this primarily for U.S. customers, or should I treat the market as global?"

Then use the answer to determine whether another question is necessary.

---

# 6. Handling Conflicting Information

When two user statements materially conflict:

1. Identify the specific conflict.
2. Ask one question that resolves it.
3. Do not make the user restate unrelated information.

Example:

"You described the service as aimed at independent dentists earlier, but the latest example focuses on hospital systems. Which customer should anchor the analysis?"

---

# 7. Decision-Specific Follow-Up Questions

After a major analytical section, the question should influence what comes next.

## Industry Overview Examples

- "Which matters more for your decision: market growth or ease of entry?"
- "Should we treat small businesses or enterprise buyers as the more relevant customer segment?"
- "Of these demand drivers, which one most closely matches the opportunity you're considering?"

## Competitor Analysis Examples

- "Which competitive dimension matters most for your decision: price, specialization, or customer experience?"
- "Should the next comparison focus on direct competitors or substitutes customers use instead?"
- "Which competitor appears closest to the position you would want to occupy?"

## Market Gaps Examples

- "Which gap looks more worth testing first: the underserved segment or the workflow inefficiency?"
- "Would you rather optimize for a larger market or a more painful niche problem?"
- "Which of these unmet needs matches a customer you can realistically reach?"

## Business Idea Fit Examples

- "Which assumption would you want to validate first: customer pain, willingness to pay, or acquisition?"
- "Is differentiation or market demand the bigger concern for you right now?"
- "Would you prefer to narrow the idea to the strongest segment or test the broader proposition first?"

---

# 8. Weak Follow-Up Patterns

Avoid questions that exist only to continue the conversation.

Examples:

- "Should I continue?"
- "Would you like more?"
- "Do you want the next section?"
- "Is this helpful?"

Use a question that helps determine the next analytical direction instead.

---

# 9. Reflective Prompts

Short reflective prompts can help surface assumptions without restarting intake.

Examples:

- "It sounds like your bigger concern is customer access rather than market size."
- "The decision seems to hinge on whether this problem is painful enough to pay for."
- "Your target customer appears narrower than the overall industry."

Use these only when supported by what the user has said.
